# Marketing copy review checklist

A general checklist for reviewing any marketing copy before it goes out. It is not tied to any brand, product or platform. It is written from the everyday common sense of advertising review: say what is true, be able to prove it, make clear when something is an ad, and don't pressure or mislead people. Where the brand has its own policy, the policy wins on anything it covers. Where it says nothing, use this checklist and your own judgement.

## How to review

1. Read the whole draft once as the person who will see it. Note the channel, because a search ad, an email, a product page and a creator post are read in different ways and carry different expectations.
2. Go through the draft sentence by sentence and ask of each one: is this a claim, a promise, a number, an opinion, or just description? Claims, promises and numbers need the most care.
3. Check every claim against what the brand can actually show. If it isn't in the brand's list of approved claims or backed by evidence you can see, treat it as unsupported.
4. Check what is missing as well as what is there: labels, conditions, small print that should sit near the claim it qualifies.
5. Read it once more for tone and style. Copy can be accurate and still sound generic, padded or written by a machine.
6. Decide. If anything would mislead a reasonable reader, break a clear rule, or embarrass the brand, it should not go out until it is fixed. If the only notes are optional polish, it can go out.

Be precise. Quote the exact words that cause the problem. Don't flag a claim the brand has said it can prove, and don't invent problems to look thorough. A false alarm costs the team time and trust just as a miss does.

## 1. Claims need evidence

- Any factual claim about the product, the company, its customers or its results must be something the brand can prove now, with evidence it already holds, not evidence it hopes to find later.
- Numbers are claims. Customer counts, review counts, star ratings, percentages, survey results, delivery times and years in business must match the evidence exactly. A number that has been rounded up, refreshed without a source, or changed from the approved figure is a problem even when it sounds modest.
- Vague statistics with no source behind them, such as "a growing community", "4 in 5 users agree" or "the majority of shoppers", shouldn't be there unless there is real data.
- Endorsements by experts or professions ("dentist recommended", "trusted by professionals", "expert approved") need real, documented endorsements from verifiable sources.
- Service promises such as next-day dispatch, no delivery charges or always being in stock are claims too. Check they match the actual terms, including thresholds and exceptions.
- Reference pricing ("save 40%", a struck-through higher price) only works if the higher price was genuinely charged for a reasonable period before the offer. If you can't see that history, flag it.
- Claims about the company itself (founding year, location, size, how products are made) must match what the brand has stated.

## 2. Comparisons and superlatives

- "Best", "number one", "leading", "most popular", "unbeatable" and similar words are claims of superiority. They need objective, current evidence, usually independent data, and they need to say what they are compared against.
- Comparisons with other products or with a whole category ("outperforms other brands", "twice as effective") need fair, like-for-like evidence. An unnamed comparison is still a comparison.
- Opinion is allowed when it's clearly personal and not presented as fact, for example a reviewer saying they like something. A brand stating it's the best is not opinion.

## 3. Health, safety and wellbeing

- Food, drink, supplements, cosmetics and similar products cannot claim to prevent, treat or cure any illness, or to restore, correct or change body functions, unless the claim is specifically authorised for that product.
- Softer health claims are just as risky: "supports joint health", "improves focus", "strengthens hair", "balances hormones", "reduces inflammation", "speeds recovery", "good for your heart".
- Testimonials can't make health claims either. If a customer quote says a product fixed a condition, the brand is making that claim by publishing it.
- Mood and lifestyle language is usually fine when it describes the experience rather than an effect on the body: "a relaxing bath" is description; "lowers your blood pressure" is a health claim.
- Safety claims ("completely safe", "no side effects", "suitable for everyone") need evidence and are often best avoided.

## 4. Environmental claims

- Green claims must be specific, accurate and provable across the whole life of what they describe. Broad terms such as "green", "environmentally friendly", "kind to nature" or "responsible" are too vague to stand on their own.
- "Net zero", "climate positive" and offsetting claims need full, current evidence and usually an explanation of how they were achieved.
- A true claim about one part of a product (the label, the lid, one ingredient) must not be stretched to cover the whole product, all of its packaging or the business.
- Don't imply a benefit that is just a legal requirement or standard practice for every product of that kind.

## 5. Promises and guarantees

- Guarantees ("money-back promise", "no-quibble returns", "results guaranteed") are binding promises. They need real terms behind them that the business has agreed to honour, and the main conditions should be clear.
- Watch for soft or implied guarantees that a customer could reasonably rely on, even when the word "guarantee" isn't used.
- Results claims ("you'll notice the difference", "transforms your routine") promise an outcome the brand can't control. Prefer describing the product.

## 6. Reviews, testimonials and social proof

- Testimonials must come from real customers, reflect their genuine experience, and be used with permission. Quotes with no traceable source should be checked before use.
- Don't paraphrase or summarise what customers say into a stronger claim unless that summary is truly representative and documented.
- Ratings and review counts must say where they come from and match the source exactly.
- Don't cherry-pick reviews in a way that gives a false impression of overall opinion.

## 7. Urgency, scarcity and pressure

- Limited-time offers must really be limited, and the end time must be true. "Final hours" or "offer ends soon" is a problem if the offer runs again or carries on.
- Stock claims ("last few remaining", "limited stock", "going quickly") must be true when the copy is seen. If the brand can't show low stock, or the phrase is decoration, flag it.
- Don't pressure people with guilt or alarm ("don't miss out or you'll regret it").

## 8. Ads, sponsorship, gifts and affiliate links

- Anything the brand paid for, or gave something in return for, must be obviously identifiable as advertising. The reader should know it's an ad before they engage with it, not after.
- Creator and influencer posts that are paid, gifted, or part of any commercial relationship need a clear, upfront label such as "Ad". A polite mention of a free product or a vague "collab" is not enough on its own.
- The label should be prominent and come first. One that is mixed in with other tags, hidden after "more", placed at the end of a long caption or only in a bio is not clear enough.
- Gifted items still need a label when the creator is posting because of the gift.
- Affiliate links need a clear disclosure the reader will see before clicking, in plain words, next to the link or at the top of the content. A disclosure tucked away elsewhere is not enough.
- Brand-owned channels (the brand's own website, emails and accounts) don't need an "Ad" label on every post, but paid placements still need to be recognisable as marketing.

## 9. Pricing, offers and terms

- Prices must be accurate and include any compulsory charges. Delivery costs and thresholds must match the actual terms.
- "Free" must really be free, with conditions stated near the claim.
- Subscription copy should make the commitment and how to cancel clear. Don't say cancelling is easy unless it is.
- Offer conditions (minimum spend, dates, exclusions) should sit close to the offer, not hidden away.

## 10. Style, tone and AI clichés

- Copy should sound like a specific person or team wrote it for a specific reader. Flag writing that sounds generic or machine-made.
- Common filler and AI phrases to cut: "look no further", "game-changer", "seamless", "curated", "delve into", "a testament to", "in the realm of", "nestled", "tapestry", "symphony of", "take it to the next level", "designed with you in mind", "we've got you covered".
- Watch for formula rather than voice: the rule of three used for rhythm alone, stacked adjectives, or a rhetorical question followed by its own answer.
- Cut empty intensifiers and hype ("truly", "incredibly", "absolutely", "like never before").
- Check for repetition: the same phrase or point made twice in a short piece.
- Check spelling and conventions for the audience (for example UK or US English) and keep them consistent.

## 11. People, privacy and fairness

- Don't use a real person's name, image or words without permission. Don't imply endorsement by someone who hasn't given it.
- Avoid stereotypes and copy that could cause offence, or that targets vulnerable people, children, or people in difficult circumstances.
- Don't make claims about competitors that could be misleading or denigrating.

## 12. Channel checks

- Search ads: every claim in a short headline or description still needs evidence. Short copy is not an excuse for shortcuts.
- Product pages: the description must match the actual product, size, contents and price.
- Emails: subject lines must not mislead about what's inside. Check sender details and any personalisation.
- Social posts: check labels, tags, hashtags and any claims in images or captions.
- Review and comparison content: make sure the relationship with the brand is disclosed and the review is honest.
- Landing pages: the headline promise must match what the page and product actually deliver.

## 13. Making the call

- Hold the draft if it contains an unsupported claim, a health claim, a vague green claim, a guarantee without terms, a misleading price, false urgency, a missing or hidden ad or affiliate label, or a testimonial you can't stand behind.
- Hold it too if it contains clichés or formulaic copy the brand's voice wouldn't use, because it will reach real readers with the brand's name on it.
- Let it through if every claim is approved or provable, labels are clear, and any remaining notes are optional polish. Say so plainly, and don't hold good copy back for the sake of it.
- When you flag something, quote the exact words, name the problem type, and suggest a fix where one is obvious.
- When you are unsure whether a claim is approved, check the brand's policy first. If the policy allows it, it is fine.
